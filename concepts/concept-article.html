Spacing is the work nobody sees

A short look at sidebearings, kerning, and the quiet labor that makes a paragraph hold together.

March 2026 · 8 min read

Most readers never notice spacing. Designers notice almost nothing else.

A typeface is not only the shapes of letters. It is the air between them — the table of sidebearings, the kerning pairs, the third revision of a lowercase g that finally sits still in a paragraph. This note walks through that invisible labor with pictures that interrupt the text and notes that sit beside it.

What follows is placeholder copy so you can judge measure, rhythm, and how notes relate to the main column. Swap it for real writing when you have an essay ready.

Sidebearings

Early versions of the g were still too wide on the right.

Sidebearings set how much empty space sits on each side of a glyph before kerning begins. Too tight and the word clenches; too loose and the line frays. The left margin holds short notes only — no pictures there.

When the same letter repeats, optical rhythm matters more than mathematical equality. Two n’s in “running” need slightly different treatment than an n beside an o. That is where kerning starts — and where most of the hours go.

Main pictures live in the text column. Use them sparingly.

Kerning

Classic pair: T and o. Notes stay text-only.

Kerning is exception handling. You fix the pairs that sidebearings cannot: T+o, L+y, f+i. Large pictures interrupt the reading column; side notes annotate it without competing for attention.

On a screen, the same decisions hold — only the resolution and the reading distance change. A text face that works at 16px still needs the discipline of print: consistent color, quiet rhythm, no sudden holes in the line.

Keep the left column short — one thought, not a second essay.

That is the template: a lead picture, then a large title and short intro, then plain text with occasional figures and left-side notes. On narrow screens, each note drops directly under the paragraph it belongs to.
